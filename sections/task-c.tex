\section{In-Context-Learning of Black-Scholes}

As an extension of the work presented in \cite{garg2022what}, we intent to test the learning-to-learn and predictive abilities of transformer on a real world financial application: the European Option Market. 

\subsection{Introduction to Black-Scholes}

Consider a stock that trades at $S = \$100$ today. A \emph{call option} on this stock
gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy the stock at a fixed
\emph{strike} price, say $K = \$100$, at a fixed \emph{maturity}, say $T = 1$ year from
now. If the stock trades at \$130 in a year, the holder buys at \$100 and gains \$30;
if it trades at \$80, the holder simply does not use the option and loses nothing more.

This right has a price $C$ today, which is much smaller than the stock price: with
$\sigma = 0.2$ and $r = 0.02$ (defined below), $C \approx \$8.9$, i.e.\ $C/S \approx 0.089$.
The price depends strongly on how much the stock is expected to fluctuate, its
\emph{volatility} $\sigma$: if the stock is twice as volatile ($\sigma = 0.4$), large gains become
more likely while losses remain capped, and the same option costs $C \approx \$16.7$.
Strike, maturity and today's price are written in the contract and observable, but the
volatility is not. In practice it is instead inferred from the prices of other options
on the same stock, which is then used to price new ones. 

This is precisely an in-context
learning problem: given a few (option, price) pairs on one stock, predict the price of
another option on that stock.

The Black-Scholes model makes this relationship precise. We define a new family of
functions, namely the set of Black-Scholes functions defined as
\begin{equation}
  \frac{C}{S} = \Phi(d_1) - e^{m - rT}\,\Phi(d_2), \qquad
  d_1 = \frac{-m + \left(r + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad
  d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T},
  \label{bs}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the price of a European call option, $S$ the spot price of the asset,
$K$ the strike, $m = \ln(K/S)$ the log-moneyness, $T$ the time to maturity in years,
$r = 0.02$ the risk-free rate, $\sigma$ the volatility and $\Phi$ the standard normal CDF.
The function is non-linear but low dimensional: two inputs $(m, T)$ and one to three
hidden parameters.

The unknown volatility $\sigma$ should be learned by the transformer from in-context
examples, and used to infer the call price of a new option on the same asset.
We consider two variations of the problem. In the first, \emph{flat volatility}, each
prompt has a constant volatility $\sigma \sim U[0.1, 0.5]$. In the second,
\emph{smile volatility},
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(m) = \max\left(\sigma_0 + b\,m + c\,m^2,\ 0.05\right), \qquad
  \sigma_0 \sim U[0.15, 0.4],\ b \sim U[-0.4, 0],\ c \sim U[0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $m = \ln(K/S)$. The latter takes into account that implied volatility observed in
markets varies with strike (skew $b$ and curvature $c$), and is therefore a more
realistic model. Here the transformer must infer three parameters from the context
instead of one.

Again, we use the same training setup as described in \cite{garg2022what}, where this
time we define the prompt as
\begin{equation}
  P_{BS} = \big(x_1, f_\theta(x_1), \dots, x_k, f_\theta(x_k), x_{\text{query}}\big),
  \qquad x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2),
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ denotes the hidden volatility parameters ($\sigma$, or $(\sigma_0, b, c)$),
drawn once per prompt. As in \cite{garg2022what}, the inputs are Gaussian; they are
mapped to option features by $m = 0.2\,x^{(1)}$ and $T = 0.1 + 1.9\,\Phi(x^{(2)}) \in [0.1, 2]$,
and the target $f_\theta(x)$ is the price $C/S$ from \eqref{bs}, standardised by its mean
and standard deviation under the prior, so that predicting the mean gives error $\approx 1$.
Each prompt thus corresponds to one underlying: $k$ options with their prices, followed
by a new option to price.

We compare the prediction error \eqref{error} as before to the baseline calibration
method, which fits the volatility parameters to the in-context examples by least squares
and prices the query with the fitted parameters,
\begin{equation}
\hat\theta = \arg\min_{\theta'} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \big(f_{\theta'}(x_i) - y_i\big)^2,
\qquad \hat y_{\text{query}} = f_{\hat\theta}(x_{\text{query}}),
\end{equation}
where $y_i = f_\theta(x_i)$ are the prices given in the prompt. This is the optimal estimator when the volatility model is known, and is how practitioners
calibrate implied volatility. 

For the smile task we run it with both the more accurate smile model and a misspecified flat model, to test whether the transformer uses the smile. The latter then acts as a stand-in for a lazy strategy. The transformer could get a decent error on the smile task by estimating one average volatility from the examples and ignoring how $\sigma$
changes with strike. If it did that, its error would flatten out at about the flat-calibration level, $\approx 0.015$.
Since the pricing formula is the same in every prompt, a transformer could learn an
average price surface in its weights without using the context. We therefore also
report a no-context baseline, the prior-mean price
$\hat y_{\text{query}} = \mathbb{E}_\theta\big[f_\theta(x_{\text{query}})\big]$; only error
below this baseline is evidence of in-context learning of $\sigma$.

We evaluate the transformer's ability to in-context learn $\sigma$ and infer option prices
under clean prices; noisy prices, $f_\theta(x_i) + \varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$, simulating bid--ask noise (the analogue of
noisy linear regression in \cite{garg2022what}); and distribution shifts between the
in-context examples and the query, without retraining: out-of-the-money examples with an
in-the-money query, short-maturity examples with a long-maturity query, and moneyness
scaled by 2 and 3 (the analogue of input scaling in \cite{garg2022what}).

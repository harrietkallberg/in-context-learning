\section{In-Context Learning of Black--Scholes}

  As an extension of \cite{garg2022what}, we test in-context learning on a synthetic task
  modelled on a real-world financial problem: pricing European options. Financial terms are
  defined in Appendix~\ref{app:glossary}.

  \subsection{Introduction to Black--Scholes}

  A \emph{call option} gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy a stock
  with \emph{spot price} $S$ at a fixed \emph{strike} price $K$ at a fixed \emph{maturity}
  $T$ years from now. Its price $C$ today depends strongly on the stock's \emph{volatility}
  $\sigma$ (worked example in Appendix~\ref{app:call_price}). $S$, $K$ and $T$ are
  observable, but $\sigma$ is not. In practice it is inferred from the prices of other
  options on the same stock. This is an in-context learning problem:  given the strikes, maturities and prices of a few
  options on one stock, predict the price of another option on that stock.

  The Black--Scholes model makes this relationship precise. We define the family of
  Black--Scholes functions as
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{C}{S}(m, T) = \Phi(d_1) - e^{m - rT}\,\Phi(d_2), \qquad
    d_1 = \frac{-m + \left(r + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad
    d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T},
    \label{bs}
  \end{equation}
  where $m = \ln(K/S)$ is the log-moneyness, $r = 0.02$ the known \emph{risk-free rate} and $\Phi$
  the standard normal CDF. The function is non-linear but low dimensional, with two inputs
  $(m, T)$.

  The transformer must infer the volatility from the in-context examples. We consider two
  variants: (i) \emph{flat volatility}, $\sigma \sim U[0.1, 0.5]$ per prompt, and
  (ii) \emph{smile volatility},
  \begin{equation}
    \sigma(m) = \max\{\sigma_0 + b\,m + c\,m^2,\ 0.05\}, \qquad
    \sigma_0 \sim U[0.15, 0.4],\ b \sim U[-0.4, 0],\ c \sim U[0, 1],
  \end{equation}
  which reflects that implied volatility in real markets varies with strike (skew $b$,
  curvature $c$). The transformer must then infer three parameters instead of one.
\subsection{Training and evaluation setup}
\label{sec:bs-setup}

  \paragraph{Task.}
  Let $\theta$ denote the hidden volatility parameters, $\sigma$ for flat and
  $(\sigma_0, b, c)$ for smile volatility, and $\sigma_\theta(m)$ the resulting volatility.
  Prompts follow \eqref{prompt} with $D_\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{N}(0, I_2)$ as in
  \cite{garg2022what}, and $D_\mathcal{F}$ the distribution of $f_\theta$ for $\theta$ drawn
  from the prior, so each prompt corresponds to one stock with $\theta$ drawn once. The
  inputs are mapped to option features
  \begin{equation}
    m(x) = 0.2\,x^{(1)}, \qquad T(x) = 0.1 + 1.9\,\Phi(x^{(2)}) \in [0.1, 2],
    \label{features}
  \end{equation}
  which the transformer must learn during training. The target $f_\theta(x)$ is the price
  $C/S$ from \eqref{bs}, evaluated at $m(x)$, $T(x)$ and $\sigma = \sigma_\theta(m(x))$, and
  standardised by the mean and standard deviation of $C/S$ under the prior
  (Appendix~\ref{app:stand_price}). We write $y_i = f_\theta(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$ for the
  prices in the prompt, with $\varepsilon_i = 0$ unless stated otherwise, which recovers
  \eqref{prompt}.

  \paragraph{Training.}
  We use the training setup of \cite{garg2022what}, minimising \eqref{objective}, except
  that the curriculum only increases the number of examples, since $d = 2$ is fixed.

  \paragraph{Evaluation and baselines.}
  We report the error \eqref{error} without the factor $1/d$, which the standardisation
  replaces. We compare against calibration, which fits the volatility parameters to the
  in-context examples by least squares and prices the query with the fitted parameters,
  \begin{equation}
    \hat\theta = \arg\min_{\theta'} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \big(f_{\theta'}(x_i) - y_i\big)^2,
    \qquad \hat y_{\text{query}} = f_{\hat\theta}(x_{\text{query}}).
  \end{equation}
  This mirrors how practitioners calibrate implied volatility. We solve it by grid search
  followed by five rounds of local refinement. Since the pricing formula is the same in
  every prompt, a transformer could also learn an average price surface in its weights
  without using the context. We therefore report the prior-mean price
  $\hat y_{\text{query}} = \mathbb{E}_{f \sim D_\mathcal{F}}[f(x_{\text{query}})]$ as a
  no-context baseline. Only error below it is evidence of in-context learning.

  \paragraph{Noisy prices and wider moneyness.}
  As for noisy linear regression in \cite{garg2022what}, we also evaluate on noisy prices,
  with $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$, $\tau = 0.05$, added to the standardised
  prices of the examples and the query, simulating bid--ask noise. The error is then
  measured against the noisy query price $y_{\text{query}}$. Since its noise is
  unpredictable, the expected error cannot go below $\tau^2 = 0.0025$. The model is trained
  on clean prices only. Finally, as for input scaling in \cite{garg2022what}, we evaluate
  without retraining with $D_\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{N}(0, \operatorname{diag}(s^2, 1))$,
  $s \in \{2, 3\}$, i.e.\ moneyness scaled by $s$. Only 5\% of training options have
  $|m| > 0.4$. Under scaling by 3, half do, and 5\% have $|m| > 1.2$, where the model has
  practically never been trained.

\subsection{Results}

\paragraph{Flat and smile volatility.}
Without examples ($k=0$) the transformer's error matches that of the prior mean, and
after a single example it falls far below it ($3.4\cdot10^{-3}$ vs.\ $0.156$ on flat and
$1.1\cdot10^{-2}$ vs.\ $0.057$ on smile volatility; Appendix~\ref{app:bs-results}), so the
transformer uses the example. With flat volatility it stays above calibration for all
$k$; both level off after a few examples, the transformer about an order of magnitude
higher ($1.8\cdot10^{-5}$ vs.\ $1.2\cdot10^{-6}$ at $k=40$). With smile volatility it goes
far below misspecified flat calibration, which levels off early ($1.5\cdot10^{-2}$ at
$k=40$). Against smile calibration, the transformer has lower error up to $k \approx 18$
($1.2\cdot10^{-3}$ vs.\ $3.1\cdot10^{-3}$ at $k=3$), the two are comparable for
$k \approx 19$--$25$, and calibration is lower from about $k \approx 27$
($7.1\cdot10^{-6}$ vs.\ $1.5\cdot10^{-5}$ at $k=40$), as the transformer's error plateaus
(Figure~\ref{fig:bs-smile}, first panel). Its error also fluctuates less between
neighbouring $k$ than calibration's.

\paragraph{Noisy prices.}
At $k=40$ the transformer reaches $2.7\cdot10^{-3}$ on flat and $3.4\cdot10^{-3}$ on smile
volatility, against $2.5\cdot10^{-3}$ and $2.6\cdot10^{-3}$ for calibration
(Figure~\ref{fig:bs-smile}, second panel). Calibration reaches the noise floor
$\tau^2 = 2.5\cdot10^{-3}$ within sampling noise and the transformer comes close. With
few examples the transformer is ahead: at $k=1$ on smile volatility its error is $2.1\cdot10^{-2}$ against
$3.4\cdot10^{-2}$ for calibration.

\paragraph{Wider moneyness range.}
With smile volatility, $\sigma_\theta(m)$ is evaluated at larger $|m|$, so the
volatilities also leave the training range; with flat volatility $\sigma$ is unchanged.
On smile volatility (Figure~\ref{fig:bs-smile}, last two panels), at scale 2 the transformer
improves until about $k \approx 10$ ($1.8\cdot10^{-3}$) and then fluctuates around
$10^{-3}$, far below the prior mean ($0.060$). At scale 3 it improves much more slowly,
and for $k \geq 1$ its error is typically about half that of the prior mean, which itself
varies between $0.10$ and $0.15$. Smile calibration has error at most $2.3\cdot10^{-6}$
at $k=40$ under both scalings, while flat calibration is slightly worse than the prior
mean at scale 2 ($0.071$ vs.\ $0.060$) and far worse at scale 3 ($0.28$). On flat
volatility the failure is clearer: at scale 3 the transformer's error crosses the prior
mean's back and forth and is no better overall, while calibration reaches
$6.3\cdot10^{-7}$.

\subsection{Discussion}
The two baselines rule out two lazy strategies. Beating the prior mean after one example
rules out ignoring the context and predicting the price averaged over volatilities for
that option. Beating flat calibration on smile volatility rules out fitting a single
implied volatility, i.e.\ treating the smile as flat. The transformer therefore uses the
examples and adapts to the shape of the smile.

On smile volatility the transformer beats correctly specified calibration up to
$k \approx 18$. A likely explanation is that calibration fits each prompt from scratch,
whereas the transformer has seen the training distribution of
$(\sigma_0, \text{skew}, \text{curvature})$ and can use it as a prior, which stabilises
the estimate when examples are few. As $k$ grows the examples dominate, calibration
recovers the parameters through the exact formula, and the transformer is limited by its
approximation error.

Under wider moneyness, correctly specified calibration is unaffected because the exact
formula holds for any $m$. On smile volatility the transformer plateaus at $\times 2$,
and at $\times 3$ it remains below the prior mean but far above calibration; on flat it
is no better than the prior mean at $\times 3$. Flat volatility isolates the input shift,
since $\sigma$ stays in range, and fails most clearly, so unfamiliar inputs alone are
enough to break the model. This suggests that the transformer has learned an
approximation that holds within the training range rather than the Black--Scholes
formula itself. It parallels linear regression in \cite{garg2022what}, where the
transformer degrades under input scaling while least squares does not.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Each task was trained once without a fixed seed, so we cannot claim that the
transformer's advantage up to $k \approx 18$ is robust to training randomness. Without a
posterior-mean (Bayes-optimal) baseline, we cannot say how close the transformer is to
optimal, nor confirm that its advantage at small $k$ comes from using the prior. Finally,
the pricing formula and $r$ are the same in every prompt, so the formula may be stored in
the weights, and what is inferred in context may be only the volatility parameters.

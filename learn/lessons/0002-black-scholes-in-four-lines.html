Lesson 2 · about 15 minutes

Black–Scholes in four lines

Why the spot price disappears, why σ is the only thing the model has to work out, and what it feels like to calibrate it from one example yourself.

Your win for today: derive bs_call_over_spot from the textbook formula, and calibrate a hidden volatility from one option price, exactly as the Transformer must at k = 1.

1. The formula and its five inputs

Black & Scholes 1973, doi:10.1086/260062, with Merton 1973, doi:10.2307/3003143. For a gentle walk-through see the MIT lecture under "Primary source" below.

The price of a European call, the right to buy the stock at strike K at time T, is

C = S·Φ(d₁) − K·e−rT·Φ(d₂)
d₁ = [ ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)·T ] / (σ√T),   d₂ = d₁ − σ√T

Φ is the standard normal CDF. The price has five inputs:

InputMeaningCan a trader see it?
Sstock price todayyes, on the screen
Kstrikeyes, in the contract
Tyears to expiryyes, in the contract
rrisk-free rateyes, from bond prices
σvolatility of future log-returnsno: it's about the future
Black & Scholes (1973), on their formula: "the expected return on the stock does not appear in equation (13)."

Notice what's not on the list: the stock's expected return. Black and Scholes' surprising result is that you don't need it. So σ is the one number you can't observe, and that makes it the natural thing to hide in a prompt.

2. The four lines: where S goes

Divide both sides by S:

1.  C/S = Φ(d₁) − (K/S)·e^(−rT)·Φ(d₂)
2.  define m = ln(K/S), so K/S = e^m and ln(S/K) = −m
3.  d₁  = (−m + (r + σ²/2)·T) / (σ√T),   d₂ = d₁ − σ√T
4.  C/S = Φ(d₁) − e^(m − rT)·Φ(d₂)

S and K only ever appear as the ratio K/S, inside d₁ and d₂ and in front of the second term. So doubling both S and K doubles the price: check it by computing C(100, 100) = 8.916 and C(200, 200) = 17.832 at T = 1, σ = 0.2. Line 4 is our code, one-to-one (src/tasks.py:366):

d1 = (-m + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * T) / (sigma * T.sqrt())
d2 = d1 - sigma * T.sqrt()
return ndtr(d1) - torch.exp(m - r * T) * ndtr(d2)

The input count, step by step: five inputs (S, K, T, r, σ) → four after dividing by S (m, T, r, σ) → r fixed at 2% → two visible per option (m, T) and one hidden per stock (σ). That's why n_dims = 2, and why each prompt's hidden "function" is one number.

The two visible features come from the Gaussian x of the paper's sampler: m = 0.2·x₀ and T = 0.1 + 1.9·Φ(x₁) (bs_features, see glossary).

3. One price, one σ: implied volatility

Raise σ and the call gets more valuable, for every strike and maturity: more volatility means more chance of finishing deep in the money, and the downside stays capped at zero. Since the price strictly increases in σ, each observed price matches exactly one σ: its implied volatility.

Derman, Columbia E4718, lecture 1: under the model, "the Black-Scholes implied volatility of an option should be independent of its strike and expiration."

In the Black–Scholes world every option on the stock has the same implied vol. So one example tells you σ, and σ prices every other option. That's the whole of bs_flat.

Try it: you are the Transformer at k = 1

Slide σ until the model price matches the example, then lock in your price for the query. Do three stocks. Then try a hard example, a far out-of-the-money option a few weeks from expiry, and watch how wide a range of σ "matches".

On the hard example the price is almost zero for every σ in the prior. In exact maths it still determines σ, but its whole range is under 1% of S, so any rounding or noise swamps it. In training only about 2% of options are this weak (range under 1% of S); one informative example is enough (lesson 1: error 0.0034 after one example).

4. Why the smile task is harder

Derman, same lecture: "Since the crash, the volatility surface of index options has become skewed." Textbook treatment: Hull, Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives, ch. 20.

Real markets break the flat-vol rule: implied vol depends on the strike. bs_smile builds that in. Each prompt hides three numbers and the vol depends on moneyness:

σ(m) = σ₀ + b·m + c·m²,   σ₀ ~ U[0.15, 0.4], b ~ U[−0.4, 0], c ~ U[0, 1]

Here b ≤ 0 gives the skew (lower strikes, higher vol) and c ≥ 0 bends it into a smile. Now one price can't separate three unknowns: you need prices at different strikes. A model that assumes flat vol can't fit them at all, and our numbers show it: flat calibration stalls at error 0.015, while the Transformer reaches 0.000015.

5. Practice

Write C/S in terms of m, T, r and σ, including d₁ and d₂.

C/S = Φ(d₁) − e^(m − rT)·Φ(d₂), with d₁ = (−m + (r + σ²/2)T)/(σ√T) and d₂ = d₁ − σ√T.

Give the input-count chain from five inputs down to what the model sees and what it must infer.

(S, K, T, r, σ) → divide by S → (m, T, r, σ) → fix r → per option (m, T) visible, per stock σ hidden.

1. Why can we divide out S and keep only m = ln(K/S)?

S and K enter only through K/S, so C(λS, λK) = λ·C(S, K). The price clearly depends on S (try S = 200, K = 100), and σ is untouched by the division.

2. Which Black–Scholes input can a trader not read off the market or the contract?

σ describes future returns, so it has to be estimated, or backed out from option prices. That's exactly why it's the hidden quantity in our prompts.

3. Why does one example option pin down σ in bs_flat?

A strictly increasing price means each price corresponds to exactly one σ (implied volatility), and in bs_flat that σ holds for every option on the stock. The example and query have different maturities.

4. An example is a far out-of-the-money call expiring in five weeks. In practice, what does its price tell the model about σ?

Its price stays near zero across the whole prior (under 1% of S), so tiny errors swamp the signal. An at-the-money 1-year call moves about 15% of S across the same range.

5. On bs_smile data, flat calibration stalls at error 0.015 however many examples it gets. Why?

The model is misspecified: true vol varies with m, and no single σ reproduces all the prices. More examples can't fix a wrong model. Standard evals are noise-free.
